# Naples, Centro Storico: The Notes I'd Want Handed to Me

*2026-09-13 · 16 min read · [naples] [travel] [italy] [food] [art] [city-guide]*

Naples does not reward the checklist approach. The famous things are genuinely worth seeing, but they are also the things with the longest lines, and the city's real texture sits a few streets off the route everyone walks. I spent time here and kept notes, mostly so I would not have to reconstruct this from scratch next time. What follows is the version without dates attached, because almost nothing below depends on which week you turn up.

Two rules shaped the list. Booking beats queuing, always. And walking uphill is almost never the mistake; the views, the quiet, and the good food all live above the flat part of town.

## The views, ranked by what they cost you

There are three ways to see Naples from above, and they are not interchangeable.

**Largo San Martino, free.** This is the terrace at the top of the Vomero hill, in front of the Certosa, with the fortress behind you and the whole city unrolled below. You can pick out Spaccanapoli cutting the old town in half, the dome of Santa Chiara, the centro direzionale towers, the port, and Vesuvius sitting behind everything. No ticket, no gate, no hours. Go at golden hour and stay for the lights coming on.

**Castel Sant'Elmo, five euros each, or two-fifty each after four.** The star-shaped fortress right next to it, built out to six points by Pedro de Toledo in the 1530s on a design by the Spanish military engineer Pedro Luis Escrivà. The castle ramparts are open 8:30 to 19:30 every day, last entry 18:30. The full five euros covers the castle plus the Museo Novecento inside the piazza d'armi, a collection of 20th century Neapolitan art, open 9:30 to 17:00 and closed Tuesdays. The museum is the part that expires: from 16:00, and all day on Tuesdays, the ticket drops to two-fifty and buys the ramparts and the view. The view from the bastions frames the bay through the embrasures, which is a better photograph than an open terrace. In 2026 the moat around the fortress was opened to visitors for the first time, which adds a second route in and a slightly odd feeling of walking at the bottom of a castle instead of on top of it.

**Certosa di San Martino, six euros, two reduced.** The 14th century charterhouse beside the castle, and the reason to go is not only the Baroque interior. The hanging gardens and the terrace over the gulf are the actual payoff, and the Great Cloister has a balustrade lined with marble skulls, which is either a memento mori or a decorative joke and possibly both. Open 8:30 to 18:30 daily except Tuesdays, last entry 17:30, free under 18. The Belvedere terrace, the Great Cloister, and the church are on the shorter route; the presepi section and the Ottocento rooms open half an hour later.

If you want the free version with the best angle on the seafront instead: **Castel dell'Ovo** at the end of Via Partenope. Entry has always been free and unprenotable, and the Terrazza dei Cannoni gives you Vesuvius, the waterfront, Posillipo, and Capri on a clear day, all in one frame. One caveat worth checking before you walk over: the castle has been through a long restoration, and the Comune's own page has carried a closed-until-further-notice banner since 2025 while other sources list it as reopened. Look at the [Comune page](https://www.comune.napoli.it/vivere-il-comune/luoghi/castel-dellovo/) the day you plan to go. Even if the castle is shut, the walk along Via Caracciolo and around the Borgo Marinari below it is free and one of the best things in the city. **Parco Virgiliano** out on the Posillipo headland is free too, open 7:00 to 1:00, terraced, and where locals actually go for the panorama. It also has the advantage of having nobody selling you anything.

One walk worth doing instead of a funicular ride down: the **Pedamentina di San Martino**, 414 steps descending from the Certosa to Corso Vittorio Emanuele, past gardens and vegetable plots, UNESCO-listed as part of the historic centre. It exists because the Carthusian monks needed to haul building materials up the hill in the 1300s. The **Petraio**, a few hundred metres west, is the same idea with Liberty villas and narrow lanes, and it lands you near Chiaia. Both are free, both are downhill if you start at the top, and both are better than the queue at the station.

## Art spaces that do not punish you

**[Cappella Sansevero](https://www.museosansevero.it/).** The Veiled Christ, and the reason every guidebook leads with it. Via Francesco De Sanctis 19/21, open 9:00 to 19:00, closed Tuesdays, last entry 18:30. Twelve euros full price, eight for 18 to 26, six for 10 to 17, free for children under nine. The important part: **reservation is mandatory and daily entries are capped.** Tickets go on sale 60 days ahead. Do not turn up and hope. This is the single booking decision that most changes a trip to Naples.

**[Pio Monte della Misericordia](https://piomontedellamisericordia.it/info-e-orari/).** Via dei Tribunali 253, on the same street and a fraction of the attention. Caravaggio's *Seven Works of Mercy*, painted for this building in 1607 and still hanging in the chapel it was commissioned for, which is a different experience from seeing it in a museum. Monday to Saturday 10:00 to 18:00, Sunday 9:00 to 14:30.

**[Gallerie d'Italia](https://gallerieditalia.com/it/napoli), Via Toledo 177.** Intesa Sanpaolo's museum, and it holds Caravaggio's *Martirio di sant'Orsola*, his last painting, alongside *Vitalità del Tempo*, a survey of 20th century art from the group's collection curated by Luca Massimo Barbero. Tuesday to Friday 10:00 to 19:00, Saturday and Sunday 10:00 to 20:00, closed Mondays. Eight euros, four reduced, free under 26, and free on the first Sunday of the month. The building is worth ten minutes on its own.

**[MADRE](https://www.madrenapoli.it/).** The contemporary art museum on Via Settembrini 79, in Palazzo Donnaregina. The permanent first floor is the strongest argument: in 2005 the museum commissioned twelve artists to each take a room, and those rooms have not changed, so the Sol LeWitt, the Serra, the Kapoor, and the Clemente are reliably there whatever the temporary programme is doing. Open Monday to Saturday 10:00 to 19:30, Sunday 10:00 to 20:00, closed Tuesdays. Eight euros, four reduced. Weekend workshops and guided visits are included in the ticket.

**[MANN](https://www.museoarcheologiconapoli.it/orari-e-biglietti)**, the national archaeological museum at Piazza Museo 19, is the one big-ticket institution that is genuinely worth a half day rather than a rushed hour. Open 9:00 to 19:30, ticket office closes at 18:30, closed Tuesdays, and it opens instead on the Tuesdays that fall on public holidays. Twenty euros full price, two euros for 18 to 25, free under 18, and free on the first Sunday of the month. It is under long-term renovation, so rooms close on a rolling basis and the Egyptian collection, the Gabinetto Segreto, and the Magna Grecia section stay shut on the free days for crowd reasons. Check the opening schedule before you go, then pair it with a walk into the old town rather than treating it as a separate outing.

And then the free thing almost nobody does: **Galleria Umberto I**. Not an event, not a museum, just a late 19th century glass and iron arcade with a 56 metre dome, four entrances, and no ticket office. It sits opposite the Teatro San Carlo, two minutes from Piazza del Plebiscito. Walk through it as a shortcut between Via Toledo and the seafront instead of as a destination, and it works better.

## The alternative layer

**Rione Sanità** is the part of Naples that changed most in the last decade, and it is where I would send anyone who has already done the historic centre. It was the Spanish viceroys' noble quarter in the 17th century, then declined, then got rebuilt from inside by cooperatives run by people who live there. Time Out put it on its coolest neighbourhoods list in 2022 and the place has been absorbing that attention without turning into a theme park.

The [catacombs](https://catacombedinapoli.it/en/) under it, **San Gennaro** and **San Gaudioso**, are run by the La Paranza social cooperative, which employs young people from the neighbourhood as guides. Reservation is mandatory and they close on Wednesdays, generally 10:00 to 17:00. **[Ipogeo dei Cristallini](https://ipogeodeicristallini.org/)**, a Greco-Roman tomb found intact under a 17th century apartment block, including a Medusa relief, is bookable online, twelve euros with an audio guide or eighteen for the guided version, free for Sanità residents and children from 8 to 12. **[Cimitero delle Fontanelle](https://www.cimiterodellefontanelle.it/)**, the ossuary on Via Fontanelle 80, is the strangest of the three: open 10:00 to 18:00 with last entry at 17:15, closed Wednesdays, now paid and timed, prenotazione obbligatoria, free only for residents of the first two municipalità. Book it online and arrive ten minutes early.

The art in the same quarter is the part most visitors miss. **[Casa Morra](https://www.casamorra.org/)**, Salita San Raffaele 20/c, is Giuseppe Morra's contemporary art archive in Palazzo Cassano Ayerbo D'Aragona, built around Gutai, Fluxus, Viennese Actionism, and visual poetry. Tuesday to Friday 10:00 to 18:30, Saturday 10:00 to 14:00, closed Sunday and Monday. Seven euros, and thirteen for the combined ticket with the **[Museo Hermann Nitsch](https://www.museonitsch.org/)** on Vico Lungo Pontecorvo 29/d, which keeps the same hours. Note the closing days: this is a Tuesday-to-Saturday neighbourhood for art.

**Naples underground** is not a gimmick. **[Napoli Sotterranea](https://www.napolisotterranea.org/)** runs from Piazza San Gaetano 68, on Via dei Tribunali, forty metres down through Greek and Roman cisterns and the remains of a Roman theatre. Tours leave hourly from 10:00 to 18:00, nine departures a day, in Italian and English at the same times, so the language you want does not dictate when you go. The standard ticket is fifteen euros each for adults, ten for students aged 11 to 24, eight for children aged 5 to 10, and free under five. The online skip-the-line ticket costs more, eighteen euros each for adults. On Thursdays there is also a 21:00 departure, bookable only in advance and only once at least ten people sign up. At 90 minutes, it is fair. You cannot visit without a guided tour, capacity is limited, and booking ahead is strongly advised even though the door does eventually let walk-ups in. Queues can reach 90 minutes.

**The Quartieri Spagnoli** next to Via Toledo is the densest grid in the city, built in the 16th century as barracks for Spanish troops, and now the highest concentration of Maradona street art anywhere. You do not need a guide. Start at Piazza del Gesù Nuovo, walk west along Via Monteoliveto, enter at Via Pasquale Scura, go north up Via Emanuele De Deo, and come back via Via Toledo. Roughly 45 minutes without stopping. The **Pignasecca market** on the way is the oldest street market in Naples and the correct place to eat standing up.

**[FOQUS](https://www.foqusnapoli.it/)** on Via Portacarrese a Montecalvario 69 is a converted school complex in the same quarter, now a cultural foundation with a courtyard used for screenings, talks, and neighbourhood events. Worth checking what is on before you walk over.

## Things still being made by hand

The **presepe** tradition on **San Gregorio Armeno** is open all year, not just in December, and it is worth looking past the souvenir stalls at street level. **[La Scarabattola](https://www.lascarabattola.it/)** at Via dei Tribunali 50, run by the Scuotto brothers, is the most interesting of them: the traditional Nativity figures are there, but so are pieces that read as contemporary sculpture, and they exhibit and work with designers rather than only reproducing the 18th century. **Giuseppe Ferrigno** and **Marco Ferrigno** are the other two names I would go to for figures actually modelled by hand. **Antica Seteria** on the same street does hand-painted silk scarves and ties, which is the rare souvenir that does not feel like one.

For porcelain, **Via Santa Maria di Costantinopoli** is the street: Capodimonte ceramics trace back to the Real Fabbrica founded in 1743 for Charles of Bourbon, and the workshops there still paint by hand. For metal, **Borgo Orefici** behind Via Duomo is the old goldsmith quarter and still full of small jewellers and goldsmiths' benches. If you want a cameo or coral piece, the workshops of Torre del Greco outside the city are the actual source, and in town you should buy only from dealers who can tell you who cut the piece.

For the contemporary version of the same idea, **[Made in Cloister](https://www.madeincloister.com/)** at Piazza Enrico De Nicola 48 is an art, craft, and design foundation inside the restored 16th century cloister of Santa Caterina a Formiello, five euros, Wednesday to Saturday 11:00 to 19:00, Sunday 10:00 to 14:00, closed Monday and Tuesday, free under 18 and over 65. It programmes in two-year cycles and closes between exhibitions, so check the site before walking over.

**Riot Studio** in Palazzo Marigliano, Via San Biagio dei Librai 39, is the independent space to know about for design: contemporary exhibitions, design workshops, film programming, and a hidden courtyard garden with a Mimmo Paladino equestrian statue, a few steps off San Domenico Maggiore. It works with designers in residence rather than as a shop.

If your trip lines up with **[EDIT Napoli](https://editnapoli.com/)**, the editorial design fair that pairs independent makers with the city's historic venues, that is the best single date to plan around. It runs in early October, recently at La Santissima, the former Trinità delle Monache complex, and its satellite projects have been staged in Castel Sant'Elmo, the Certosa di San Martino, Villa Floridiana, and the Filangieri museum.

## The food list that survives repeat visits

Naples has an enormous number of places to eat and a small number that are genuinely good rather than merely famous, and the two sets overlap less than you would hope.

**Pizza a portafoglio**, the folded two-euro slice you eat walking: **Di Matteo**, Via dei Tribunali 94, open late, still the benchmark.

**Pizza fritta**, the fried stuffed version invented because the families who ate it had no oven: **Antica Pizza Fritta da Zia Esterina Sorbillo** on Via dei Tribunali 35 and Via Toledo 253, and **Starita** up in Materdei, which is where the dish got its cinematic moment in *L'oro di Napoli*. Eat it immediately and standing up. It does not travel.

**Sit-down pizza**: **L'Antica Pizzeria Da Michele** is worth doing once, and the process is that you take a numbered ticket from the person at the door before you queue. **Sorbillo** on Via dei Tribunali is the famous one and the queue reflects that; the same family's smaller outposts in the Quartieri Spagnoli are the smarter move on a busy evening.

**Sfogliatella**: **Pintauro** on Via Toledo, buy it hot. **Babà**: **Scaturchio** on Spaccanapoli. **Fiocco di neve**, the ricotta cream brioche, is a Sanità speciality and **Poppella** is where it comes from. **Taralli** from any panificio in the old town, with a beer.

**Fried fish**: the **Porta Nolana** market, in a paper cone, is the best version. **Friggitoria Fiorenzano** at Piazza Montesanto is the reference point for mixed fried food. The rule for friggitorie is volume: high turnover means fresh oil.

**Trattoria da Nennella**, Vico Lungo Teatro Nuovo 103-105, is a full Neapolitan experience rather than a quiet meal. They shout, they sing, the tables are shared, it is cash only, and it is lunch only Monday to Saturday. Roughly fifteen euros.

Two habits worth adopting. Coffee costs less if you drink it standing at the bar, and the price difference is not small. And carry cash, because a lot of the good small places do not want your card.

## Logistics that actually matter

**Book the Cappella Sansevero and the catacombs before you arrive.** Both are capped, both take online reservations, and both sell out. Everything else on this list you can decide on the day.

**The [Campania Artecard](https://www.campaniartecard.it/)** is worth doing the arithmetic on. If you plan three or more major sites in a few days it usually wins, and it often includes transport.

**Get around on [Line 1](https://www.anm.it/index.php?lang=en) and the funiculars.** The Toledo metro station has the Robert Wilson blue mosaic installation, which is free and takes two minutes. The Montesanto, Chiaia, and Central funiculars climb the Vomero hill faster than any road route. Walking down and riding up is the best combination.

**The first Sunday of the month** is free entry at state museums in Italy, including Castel Sant'Elmo, the Certosa, MANN, and Capodimonte. The Gallerie d'Italia does the same. It is a real saving and a real crowd, so pick which of those two you prefer.

**Check opening days before you build a route.** Tuesday is the dangerous day: the Cappella Sansevero, the Certosa di San Martino, Castel Sant'Elmo's museum, MADRE, and the MANN all close on it. Wednesday takes out the catacombs and the Cimitero delle Fontanelle, Monday closes the Gallerie d'Italia, and Casa Morra and Made in Cloister both skip Sunday and Monday (Made in Cloister also takes Tuesday). Most of the Chiaia commercial galleries (Alfonso Artiaco, Fonti, Tiziana Di Caro, Studio Trisorio) close Sunday and Monday. If you want a single safe day for the whole list, aim for Thursday, Friday, or Saturday, and know that state museum hours shift with the season, so confirm the day and closing time on the [Musei Italiani](https://www.museiitaliani.it/) portal rather than trusting any list, including this one.

**The MANN runs evening openings in autumn**, with music and installations in the galleries, and the Museo e Real Bosco di Capodimonte gives you a large park you can walk for free even when the museum is shut.

## What I would skip

The big commercial galleries in Chiaia are worth an hour if you care about contemporary art, but they are white rooms in a wealthy neighbourhood and they are not what makes Naples interesting. Capodimonte is excellent and also a twenty minute bus ride out, so it is a half day rather than a stop. And the presepi shops on San Gregorio Armeno that sell plastic figurines of politicians are not the tradition; the workshops listed above are.

The city rewards the second visit more than the first, which is the honest reason I wrote this down.
